# Reflection: environmental impacts of data centers and AI

I  finished reading a paper about Google fighting to pull millions of gallons of water from an aquifer in South Carolina to cool one of its data centers. The whole thing took three years, and guess who won? Google, which is not surprising at all.

The thing that stuck with me is how the paper frames data centers as not just "where the internet lives" (from Google's own marketing line) but as actual physical things that need land, electricity, and massive amounts of water. We talk about the cloud like it's floating somewhere. It's not. It's in Berkeley County, and it's thirsty.

The authors introduce this idea called an "agri-cultural" approach, comparing data centers to farms. Like agriculture, data centers extract resources, irrigate (with water), and cultivate something (in this case, data). Honestly, I thought this metaphor was a bit of a stretch. The paper admits it's not one-to-one—no seasons, no crop rotation—so what does the dash in "agri-culture" actually do? It feels more like a clever hook than a real analytical tool. But I get the point: both farming and server farms depend on the earth, and both change local ways of life.

What really got me thinking was the power dynamic. Google kept saying, "We employ 400 people and invested $1.8 billion. We're your neighbors." Are they though? From a Marxist angle, no. A corporation isn't a neighbor. Neighbors can't pack up and leave when tax breaks expire. Neighbors don't have shareholders. The paper showed how economic arguments always won—jobs, investment, growth—over cultural ones like "people need to drink and farm." Local journalists and water managers raised alarms, but they got sidelined.

This made me think about AI too. We talk about training large language models, but where do those servers sit? What are they drinking? Every time I run a query, somewhere a data center is using water to cool down. That's not abstract. That's someone's aquifer.

The discussion on class also brought up lateral politics. States like Utah and New Mexico are practically begging big tech to come in, offering huge tax breaks. But poverty and lack of education don't magically disappear. So who actually benefits? Not the guy on a private well whose water table drops.

I want to learn more about alternatives. The paper briefly mentions surface water as an option if Google's permit was denied. Why wasn't that pursued? Is it cost? Politics? Technical limits?
