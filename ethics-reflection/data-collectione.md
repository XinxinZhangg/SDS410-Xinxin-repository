# Reflection: Data Collection 

## 1. What is the main idea of this article?

The main idea of this article is that data collection in machine learning is often unsystematic and resembles a wild west environment, lacking clear standards or ethical oversight. The authors argue that artificial intelligence researchers should learn from archival science, a field that has long experience managing human data responsibly. Archives have developed structured approaches over decades to handle issues such as consent regarding how data is gathered from people, inclusivity which focuses on representing diverse groups, power dynamics that determine who controls the data, transparency through documenting how data is collected, and ethics and privacy to protect sensitive information. The key argument is that machine learning should adopt more interventionist data practices, meaning that researchers should actively design datasets with ethical goals in mind rather than passively scraping massive amounts of data from the internet without consideration for the consequences. The article challenges the common assumption that simply collecting more data leads to better artificial intelligence and instead proposes that the quality, context, and ethical foundation of data are far more important than sheer quantity. 

## 2. What did I learn from this article?

I thought artificial intelligence problems such as bias or unfair outcomes as mainly caused by algorithms or model design. This reading shifted my perspective by demonstrating that data is actually the foundation of these issues. I learned that artificial intelligence systems are not neutral because the data they are trained on reflects historical inequalities and social biases that exist in the real world. If datasets are collected from the internet without careful consideration, they will inevitably overrepresent certain groups such as younger, urban, or male users while simultaneously underrepresenting others including older adults, rural populations, and women. This article taught me that artificial intelligence is not just a technical system but rather a social system that encodes human values, power structures, and cultural assumptions. I also learned that data collection is not a neutral or purely mechanical step in the machine learning pipeline because it involves power, choices, and values at every stage. Improving artificial intelligence requires not only better models but also better data practices that prioritize ethics and accountability. That being said, I also developed a critique of the article itself. I felt that the paper seems to idealize archival systems as a perfect solution to the problems facing machine learning. Although archives provide useful models, they are not free from bias or power imbalances themselves. Historically, archives have also excluded marginalized voices and reflected dominant political or cultural perspectives. This raises the important question of whether simply borrowing from archival practices is sufficient or whether deeper structural changes are needed to address the black box behind data collection and curation.

## 3. What did I learn and inspire from class?

From class discussion, I learned an additional insight that deeply resonated with me. One key point that stood out from our conversation was that there is no justification for why having a massive amount of data is automatically better or more desirable. The assumption that bigger datasets always lead to better artificial intelligence needs to be questioned because simply collecting more data does not solve ethical problems and can in fact amplify existing biases and inequalities. What inspired me most from both the article and the class discussion is the possibility of building artificial intelligence systems differently through community participation, shared governance, and interventionist data practices. I also want to understand how feasible these ethical data collection practices are in real world industry settings where efficiency and profit often take priority over social responsibility.
